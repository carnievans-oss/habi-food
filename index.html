📋 Collection Summary
Visual reference for anyone collecting browse — carer or volunteer. How to identify each food tree, signs of stress or disease, and when to leave the branch on the tree.
⚠ Universal signs — never collect if you see these
🚫 Oily or waxy film on leaves — chemical treatment. Walk away, note location, report to carer.
🚫 Unusually strong or chemical smell — herbicide or pesticide application. Do not collect.
🚫 Orange or yellow powdery pustules on leaf undersides — Myrtle rust. Affects all Myrtaceae (eucalypts, melaleucas, paperbarks). Do not collect. Report.
🚫 Distorted, thickened or abnormally coloured new growth — possible fungal infection or severe pest load.
🚫 Foam, unusual deposits or residue on leaves or bark — unknown origin. Avoid.
🚫 Freshly cut stumps or obvious recent pruning nearby — possible recent council treatment. Collect from unaffected branches only.
🟡 Brown or crispy leaf tips only — drought stress on tips. Collect from unaffected branches further from tips. Animal should be offered moist alternatives alongside.

ℹ️ About HabiFood

Mission Statement
"The right information, at the right time, for the people who do the actual work — so the work is worth doing for the rest of their lives."
HabiFood exists to reduce the hidden costs — in time, money, and human welfare — that make wildlife rehabilitation in Australia unsustainable for the volunteers who carry almost all of it.

🌿 Why this platform exists

Wildlife rehabilitation in Victoria is carried out overwhelmingly by licensed volunteers who fund rescues from their own income, run full-time shelters alongside full-time jobs, and have almost no shared infrastructure. The result is a system where individual carers spend hours driving to find a single species of gum tree — a task that, with access to the right data, should take seconds.

Natural food sourcing is so labour-intensive that carers keep their knowledge private — not out of malice, but because finding a reliable patch of Eucalyptus viminalis near a possum release site is genuinely hard, and sharing it feels like giving away a scarce resource. This hoarding is rational at the individual level and catastrophic at the sector level.

The human cost is not discussed publicly because carers don't stop to discuss it — they're too busy. A planned burn happens Tuesday. Nobody told the carers. The wombats and wallabies arrive Friday. The carer cancels a family event they'd had for three months. Again. The animals in care that week don't get walked because there's no time. The relationship that was already under strain takes another hit. This is not an edge case. This is the operational reality for the majority of active rehabilitators in Victoria — and it is the primary reason experienced carers leave the sector. Not the animals. Not the emotional weight. The inability to plan anything, because the information that would allow planning is sitting in a council works system, a DEECA permit database, or an FFMVic burn schedule — and no one has ever connected it to the people who need it.

Meanwhile, carers from different organisations don't communicate. Release sites get quietly overcrowded. Animals from the same road accident get reported to three different agencies. Planned burns and habitat clearing near release sites go unnoticed until animals are already there. None of this is anyone's fault — there has simply never been a shared platform.

HabiFood is that platform.

🎯 What HabiFood is — and what it deliberately isn't

Wildlife rehabilitation databases already exist. Teams have built them, organisations have tried to maintain them, and the pattern is always the same: the daily data entry requirement — condition scores, treatment logs, weight records, feeding notes — doesn't survive contact with the reality of running a shelter while working full time. The data goes stale, the system gets abandoned, and carers go back to notebooks and memory.

HabiFood doesn't try to fix that problem. It solves a different one — the one nobody has touched. The browse. The habitat. The government data that directly affects both. The volunteer collection sheet that means the carer doesn't have to do every single run themselves. The burn warning that arrives before the animals do. The release site assessment that doesn't require a site visit. None of that exists anywhere else. That's this.
HabiFood works alongside existing wildlife management systems — it doesn't replace them. For carers who use WIRES, WildlifeTracker, or other databases for their care records, HabiFood is the layer those systems don't have: food, habitat, and what's about to happen in your area.
1 · Find browse and assess release sites without leaving the shelter
GPS-locate the nearest food tree for any species, generate a printable volunteer collection sheet, assess a release site from a map tap. None of this requires another carer's data — it runs on government tree registers, vegetation mapping, and EVC species metadata.
2 · Know what's coming before it arrives
Planned burn in 9 days means a surge of wombats in 2 weeks. Council spraying a roadside on Tuesday means that eucalyptus patch is off-limits for 6 weeks. A clearing permit approved last month means that habitat is gone before the animals know it. HabiFood surfaces this from government data automatically — no carer entry required.
3 · Two data points that require almost nothing from carers
When a carer logs a rescue — species, suburb, cause, date, 30 seconds — it builds a shared density map that shows every carer in the network where animals are being found and why. When they log a release — species, location, habitat — it prevents invisible overcrowding of the same release zones by carers who don't know each other. These two minimal entries, done once and never updated, are the only carer contribution the network needs to function.

🔒 Privacy by design — the non-negotiable

What is never shared with other carers
❌ How long an individual animal is in care
❌ Care methods, feeding protocols, or treatment approaches
❌ Individual animal outcomes
❌ Shelter throughput, capacity, or activity level
❌ Any data that describes how a specific carer operates

This isn't a technical limitation — it's a deliberate design decision. The wildlife rehabilitation sector carries structural tensions between carers, organisations, and agencies. Data that profiles individual practice can be used with malicious intent regardless of how it was intended. HabiFood does not provide those tools. It never will.

What is shared — and why it's safe
✅ Rescue density — species, approximate location, cause. Nobody knows who logged it.
✅ Release density — species, location grid, habitat type. Nobody knows whose animal it was.
✅ Browse stage — private to the carer only. Never leaves your device.
✅ Government data — burns, chemicals, clearings. Nobody logged it — it flows automatically.

The two shared data points — rescue and release density — are anonymous by default, location-gridded (not exact address), and contain nothing about the carer. What they build, in aggregate, is a picture of where animals are and where they're going that benefits every carer in the network equally — including the ones who contributed nothing.

🔬 Methods and limitations

Food tree suitability is derived strictly from EVC dominant canopy species metadata and documented foraging ecology of each species. No habitat modelling is applied beyond what the EVC framework already encodes. No claims are made about wildlife population density beyond what vegetation structure allows. No Atlas of Living Australia data is used.

Release site suitability indicators (over-release pressure, planned burn proximity, development overlays) are informational starting points only. Decisions about individual animal releases remain the responsibility of the licensed rehabilitator.

🧑‍⚕️ Carer Sustainability — the platform's fourth purpose

The three operational purposes of HabiFood — food sourcing, release success, and sector co-operation — have a fourth underlying purpose that is never stated anywhere because wildlife rehabilitation has never had a platform to state it: keeping the people sustainable.

Victoria's licensed wildlife rehabilitation sector is carried by a small number of highly skilled volunteers who fund their own operations, provide care that would cost the public health system thousands of dollars per animal if it had to be formalised, and receive almost no systemic support in return. The average active rehabilitator operates a shelter at personal expense while maintaining employment. The cost — financial, physical, and relational — is not sustainable at scale, and the evidence is in the attrition rate: experienced carers leave, new carers don't stay, and the animals who would have been saved by the people who burned out are the silent statistic that no data system currently captures.

HabiFood cannot solve the funding problem. It cannot reduce the emotional weight of intensive care for injured animals. What it can do is remove the operational friction — the hours of reactive scrambling, the midnight food runs, the family events cancelled because a burn nobody announced generated thirty rescues overnight — and return that time and predictability to carers as a resource they can use to remain in the sector for the long term.

The animals need these people. The information exists to support them better. This platform connects the two.

The problem that isn't talked about
Wildlife rehabilitation in Victoria operates in a permanent state of reactive crisis. Not because carers aren't skilled or dedicated — because there is no system that tells them what's coming. A planned burn. A land clearing job starting Monday. An arborist removing six hollow-bearing trees on Thursday. Each one is a guaranteed surge of animals requiring immediate intensive care. Each one arrives without warning. Each one disrupts everything else: work, family, health, finances. The data to give carers advance notice of every single one of these events already exists, is already collected, and is already sitting in a council or agency database. It just isn't connected to the people whose lives it directly controls.
Arborist Work Orders & Significant Tree Removal Permits
All 79 Victorian LGAs · Private arborist companies · VicRoads · Parks Victoria
This is the most immediately actionable dataset in this entire document. When an arborist removes a hollow-bearing tree, wildlife inside — brushtail possums, ringtail possums, sugar gliders, feathertail gliders, owls, kookaburras — are displaced or trapped instantly. Carers are called after the tree is already on the ground. With 48-hour advance notice from a work order notification, carers can pre-stage a response: get boxes to the site before felling begins, arrange to be present during removal, ensure hollows are checked systematically rather than reactively. Council arborists already require permits for significant tree removal — that permit is generated in a works management system days or weeks before work begins. A spatial notification from that system to HabiFood is a single API endpoint that would directly save animal lives and reduce emergency call-outs for carers.
Request: Tree removal work order — species, estimated hollow status (if assessed), location, scheduled date. From existing council works management systems. 48-hour advance notification preferred. No private property owner details.
What the notification enables: Pre-purchase possum boxes and joeys pouches. Contact Wildlife Victoria to stage response. Arrange a second carer to attend the site. Tell employer/family in advance. Arrange vet pre-notification for potential crush injuries. None of this is possible without notice. All of it is possible with 48 hours.

This is a guaranteed mass-casualty event for the local carer network. The clearing notice or permit to remove native vegetation exists as a record with DEECA weeks before clearing begins. Request: Burn polygon, planned ignition date, vegetation type, approximate area. From existing FFMVic burn planning systems — the burn plan already contains all of this. We need it in a spatial API format rather than a PDF calendar.
New Planting Programs — revegetation, street trees, parks developments
LGAs · CMAs · Parks Victoria · Melbourne Water · Carbon project proponents
New plantings are the good news side of the operational equation — and equally invisible to carers. A council planting 200 Eucalyptus viminalis along a creek corridor in Ringwood in March means that by 2029 there's a significant food resource that doesn't exist today. If carers know about it at planting, they can record it in HabiFood as a "future food source" and begin planning releases into that corridor 4–5 years ahead. This is the difference between reactive and managed — but it requires knowing what was planted, where, and when.
Request: Planting location, species, quantity, date. From existing council horticulture and revegetation project records.
5-year impact: Carers begin planning release sites and food corridors around known future canopy. Councils can be shown in real time whether their plantings are being used — both by carers and by the wildlife they release. A native tree in a park is no longer an invisible infrastructure asset. It has a documented ecological function and a documented user community.
Emergency / Reactive Events — floods, extreme weather, infrastructure failure
VicEmergency · SES · Bureau of Meteorology · EPA
Floods displace ground-dwelling species and create acute rescue surges. Extreme heat events — heatwaves above 40°C — kill flying foxes en masse and cause dehydration crises in possums, koalas, and wombats. Hailstorms strip canopy food sources overnight. Infrastructure failures that close roads or restrict movement affect carer response capability. BOM already issues these alerts — VicEmergency already maps active events. Integration into HabiFood would mean carers receive a single operational notification that combines the weather event, its likely wildlife impact, and the current capacity state of the carer network in the affected area.
Request: VicEmergency and BOM alert polygons integrated via existing public APIs — both already publish machine-readable alert feeds. Requires only a structured interpretation layer to translate weather event type into likely wildlife impact.
Private Arborist Scheduling — voluntary industry notification
Arboriculture Australia · ISA Certified Arborists · Private contractors
Not all tree removals require council permits — particularly on private land under exemptions. A voluntary notification system — where arborists log a planned hollow-bearing tree removal in HabiFood 48 hours before felling — would extend the advance notice system to private work. Many arborists are already trained to check hollows and want to do the right thing. They simply have no system to notify carers efficiently. A simple web form or app submission from arborists would create a notification that reaches every HabiFood-registered carer in the area instantly.
Request: Voluntary. Tree species (if known), hollow assessment (yes/no/unknown), address (suburb level only for privacy), planned date. No property owner details.
What operational intelligence returns to carers
Time. Not scouting for food. Not scrambling for supplies at midnight. Not calling around to find backup. Time that currently goes to reactive crisis management would be available for the animals already in care, for relationships, for rest, for the things that make it sustainable to keep doing this.

Predictability. A planned burn on the 15th means a supply run on the 10th. An arborist job on Thursday means a second carer arranged by Tuesday. A development clearing next month means a rest week before it hits. None of this requires more carers, more money, or more government support. It requires the data that already exists to reach the people who need it.

Sustainability. The single most common reason experienced carers leave is not the cost, not the animals, and not the emotional weight. It's the inability to plan anything. Relationships, family, personal health — all sacrificed not because the animals demand it, but because the system demands it. Information is the intervention. Not funding, not recognition, not policy. Just: knowing what's coming, with enough time to prepare.
What every agency and council receives in return
🌳 For councils — Native Food Tree Gap Report
A suburb-by-suburb map showing which native food tree species are absent or under-represented in the public tree canopy relative to current wildlife rescue populations in that LGA. Directly usable in:
• Urban forest strategy updates
• Development permit landscaping conditions
• Requirements to retain or replace specific food tree species
• Minimum percentage native species in new subdivisions
📊 For DEECA — Rescue & Release Intelligence
Aggregate rescue density by species, suburb, and cause — across the entire licensed carer network — in real time. No other system provides this. This data directly supports:
• Permit allocation (which species, which regions need more carers)
• Infrastructure risk assessment (rescue hotspot = infrastructure hazard)
• Population monitoring without formal surveys
• Evidence base for habitat protection decisions
🔥 For FFMVic & CFA — Ground Truth After Burns
Post-burn animal displacement patterns, species arrival in surrounding rescue data, and vegetation recovery indicators — all from a network operating on the ground faster than formal ecological surveys. The carer network is the most distributed ecological monitoring system in Victoria. It just hasn't been treated as one.
🏗 For Planning Authorities — Wildlife Impact Evidence
Rescue frequency by species and suburb, available as structured evidence for development assessment. Any proposed clearing or development in a high-rescue postcode can be assessed against a real-world impact dataset — not just desktop habitat modelling. This is evidence that currently doesn't exist in a form planning authorities can use.
💊 For councils — Chemical Safety Verification
Councils providing chemical treatment records receive, in return, verification that their records are being actively used to protect wildlife. This is a direct, measurable biodiversity outcome from an existing compliance workflow — at zero additional cost. It's the kind of data-to-outcome connection that supports council biodiversity strategy reporting and community engagement.
The fundamental shift
Wildlife carers are not a service that agencies provide information to. They are the most distributed, most cost-effective, most knowledgeable ecological monitoring network in Victoria — and they fund it themselves. Every agency listed in this document is producing data that directly affects the outcomes of that network. Connecting those data streams is not a favour to carers. It is the most efficient use of public sector data infrastructure that these agencies could make for the lowest cost possible: one API endpoint, already built, already maintained, never turned off.

🔬 Food Suitability Methodology
1. Source EVC polygons from DEECA NatureKit (authoritative, unmodified)
2. Map EVC code → dominant canopy genera using DEECA EVC metadata tables
3. Cross-reference genera against HabiFood food lists (minimum 10 items per animal, peer-reviewed sources)
4. Assign suitability score 1–5 per EVC polygon per animal — weighted by dietary importance
5. No predictive modelling — score is a direct count/weighting of known food species present in EVC community description

Literature basis: Fowler 2012 Nutrition for Life; Poolman 2017 Ringtail Husbandry Guidelines; ERS Care Sheets; Casey/Cardinia Indigenous Plant Guide; Collecting Browse for Wildlife QLD 2020; Cowan brushtail possum diet study; Dettmann et al. 2001; Kerle 2001; Lee & Martin 1988; DEECA EVC benchmark tables.
