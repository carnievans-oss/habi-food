Two jobs, done properly

Rescue and rehabilitation are not the same thing

The wildlife sector often blurs these together. We keep them separate because they need different skills, permits and follow-through — and because the public deserves to know exactly who does what.

01 — Rescue

Getting to the animal

  • Responding to a report of a sick, injured or orphaned native animal
  • Safe capture, containment and transport
  • Getting it to a vet or registered carer fast
02 — Rehabilitation

Getting it home

  • Ongoing care, feeding and medical support under permit
  • Building fitness and natural behaviour back up
  • Assessing, then releasing to suitable habitat

Who we are

Totally Untamed Wildlife Rescue & Rehabilitation

We're a native wildlife rescue and rehabilitation service based in Hampton Park, Victoria. Totally Untamed is privately self-funded, supported by limited grants and community donations — not by industry funding or coordinated sector services.

That independence is the point. It lets us focus on the animals in front of us, tell the truth about the state of the sector, and build the practical fixes that larger, slower structures never get to.

Native injured wildlife

Found an animal in trouble? Start here

Simple, calm steps for the public — plus fact sheets on common species and a plain guide to who does what in an emergency.

What to do

If you find sick, injured or orphaned wildlife

Your goal is to keep the animal warm, dark, quiet and contained — then get expert advice quickly. Only handle an animal if it is safe for both of you.

Keep yourself safe

Never approach snakes, large birds of prey, flying foxes, raptors or adult kangaroos. For these, call for help and keep your distance.

Contain gently

If safe, place the animal in a ventilated box lined with a towel. Cover with a light cloth. Handle as little as possible.

Warm, dark and quiet

Put the box somewhere quiet away from pets, children and noise. No radio, no peeking. Stress kills wildlife.

Do not feed or give water

The wrong food or water can be fatal, and can cause choking or aspiration. Leave feeding to trained carers.

Call for advice straight away

Phone Wildlife Victoria on (03) 8400 7300, or your local vet. Note exactly where you found the animal.

Record the location

Most native animals must be released where they were found. Drop a pin or write down the address before you move it.

Do

  • Keep it warm, dark, quiet and contained
  • Note the exact location found
  • Call a professional before acting
  • Get it to a vet or carer quickly

Don't

  • Offer food or water
  • Handle more than necessary or "check on it"
  • Try to raise or keep it yourself
  • Relocate an echidna — a puggle may be nearby

Who to call in Victoria

Statewide emergency response and volunteer dispatch for sick, injured and orphaned wildlife.

(03) 8400 7300

Whale & Dolphin Emergency

For entangled, stranded or injured whales and dolphins, call immediately.

1300 136 017

DEECA

During declared emergencies and bushfires, wildlife injuries can be reported to DEECA via the Wildlife Emergency App or phone. DEECA can also help you find a registered rehabilitation organisation.

Your local vet

Many vet clinics accept sick and injured wildlife free of charge as first-response medical care. Call ahead before you arrive.

In a life-threatening situation involving people, always call 000 first.

Fact sheets

Common species & what the public can do

Quick guidance for the animals we see most. When in doubt, contain and call — don't wait.

Fact sheet

Brushtail Possum

Common issues: orphaned joeys, car strikes, dog attacks, roof evictions.
You can: check the pouch of any dead female, keep joeys warm and dark, and never relocate a healthy possum — it's illegal and usually fatal.

Fact sheet

Magpie

Common issues: grounded fledglings, entanglement, cat attacks, swooping-season injuries.
You can: leave a healthy hopping fledgling with parents nearby; intervene only if it's injured, bleeding or a cat is involved (which always needs a vet).

Fact sheet

Short-beaked Echidna

Common issues: car strikes, dog attacks, being "rescued" unnecessarily.
You can: never dig one out or move it far — a puggle may be in a burrow. If injured, guide it into a deep, ventilated container and call for advice.

Fact sheet

Freshwater Turtle

Common issues: road crossings in breeding season, dog attacks, cracked shells.
You can: help it across in the direction it was heading; for any injury, keep it dry, dark and contained (not in water) and call a carer — shell fractures are treatable.

Fact sheet

Raven

Common issues: fledglings on the ground, netting entanglement, poisoning.
You can: keep dogs and cats away, note the location, and call for advice; ravens are highly intelligent and stress easily, so minimal handling matters.

General

Any other native animal

The same rules apply: warm, dark, quiet, contained — no food or water — and call Wildlife Victoria on (03) 8400 7300 for tailored advice before you act.

Who does what

The roles of different shelters & organisations

Knowing who's responsible for each step saves precious time in an emergency.

Emergency line

Wildlife Victoria

Takes public reports 24/7, gives advice, and dispatches trained volunteer rescuers. Usually your first call.

Government

DEECA

Authorises wildlife carers and shelters, sets the rules, and coordinates wildlife response during declared emergencies and bushfires.

Welfare & cruelty

RSPCA Victoria

Handles animal cruelty and welfare complaints and offers advice; not the primary wildlife rescue dispatcher.

Medical

Vets

First-response medical treatment and triage. Many treat wildlife at no cost to the person who found it.

Rescue

Rescuers & transporters

Get to the animal, contain it safely, and move it to a vet or carer. This is rescue — the getting-there job.

Rehabilitation

Registered shelters & carers

Provide the ongoing, permitted care that builds an animal back up for release. This is rehabilitation — the getting-home job.

Projects

Building the fixes the sector is missing

Practical projects, briefs and ways to support the work — plus how to reach us.

Project · brief

HabiFood

A habitat-food network connecting carers and rehabilitators with a reliable, mapped supply of pesticide-free native browse and food sources. The aim: stop carers scrambling for the right food, reduce waste, and put the right browse in the right hands at the right time.

The problem

Food is a scramble

Sourcing safe, species-correct browse is a constant, invisible burden on carers.

The idea

A shared network

Match surplus native greenwaste and food plants with the carers who need them.

Status

In development

Early build stage. Want the full brief or to get involved? Get in touch below.

Project · brief

Research study

A grounded research effort capturing real rehabilitation outcomes — intake causes, care approaches, release success and survival — to build evidence the sector can actually use to improve welfare and coordination.

Why

Decisions need data

Too much of the sector runs on anecdote. Consistent records change that.

What

Outcomes, not just intake

Following animals through to release and beyond, ethically and rigorously.

Status

Scoping

Framework in progress. Researchers and carers welcome to collaborate.

Project · system

Possum Box mounting system

A safe, standardised system for installing and mounting possum nest boxes — including release protocols, a documented risk assessment and a Safe Work Method Statement for emergency removals and releases. Built so the job is repeatable, compliant and safe for both people and animals.

Standardised & documented

Consistent mounting, siting and release steps rather than ad-hoc improvisation.

Risk-assessed

Backed by a formal risk-assessment matrix and Safe Work Method Statement covering installation, release and emergency removal.

Facts about wildlife rehab

Rehabilitation is not keeping wild animals as pets.
The entire goal is release. Animals are kept wild, handling is minimised, and tameness is treated as a problem to avoid.
Most native animals must be released where they were found.
It's usually a legal requirement — they know that territory, its food and its dangers.
Feeding the wrong food can kill.
Bread, milk and human food cause serious harm. Diets are species-specific and prepared by trained carers.
Carers are largely unpaid and self-funded.
Food, medical care and enclosures come out of carers' own pockets and community donations — rarely from industry funding.
